# TryFit trades away speed to control cost

TryFit (storefront-branded "TheFitSnap") enters a Shopify virtual try-on category that is young, fragmented, and technically homogeneous — most of its "competitors," including one of the two best-reviewed apps in the space, run the same class of commodity diffusion API TryFit itself uses, so raw render quality is not a defensible advantage for anyone. TryFit's genuine, provable strengths are narrow and specific: garment-type-aware prompting (e.g., saree and flat-lay handling) in a category where every other vendor treats try-on as one-size-fits-all, and — unusually for this space — an actual measured understanding of its own system's behavior under load, which no competitor has published or, based on public reviews, even been asked about. Its clearest, most honest weakness is also concrete and self-inflicted: the RunPod endpoint backing generation is deliberately capped at `workersMax: 1`, and a controlled load test found that this produces a hard first-in-line queue — a solo shopper resolves in about 15 seconds, but each additional shopper generating at the same moment adds roughly 15–20 seconds more (30.7s for a 2nd shopper, 45.3s for a 3rd at the API layer alone; 44s and 64s once real image upload, Shopify App Proxy, and Remix↔PHP round-trip overhead is included). This is not an unsolved engineering problem — the team raised `workersMax` to 10, watched it work, and reverted it specifically to protect against RunPod's per-worker billing, meaning the queueing behavior shoppers experience today is the result of an explicit cost decision, not a technical ceiling. That distinction matters enormously for how the team should talk about it internally and, if it ever comes up, externally.

## A crowded, young category where nobody has built a moat yet

The Shopify virtual try-on category is not dominated by one or two entrenched leaders; it is a long tail of mostly 2025–2026-launched apps competing on nearly identical language. Reconstructing the category from individual listings (Shopify's own category page returned a 404 during research) surfaces **Genlook, Antla, Camweara, Icona, Nivera, Aiuta, Lit Outfit, and Tryvia** as the most visible apparel-focused entrants, with Visualize AI, AI Stylist, A&A Virtual Try-On, and PictoFiT as secondary/borderline entrants ([Genlook](https://apps.shopify.com/genlook-virtual-try-on); [Antla](https://apps.shopify.com/antla); [Camweara](https://apps.shopify.com/camweara)). Review volume across the entire set is thin: only three apps clear even a modest 10-review threshold — Camweara (61 reviews, ~4.9★), Antla (57 reviews, 5.0★), and Genlook (38 reviews, 5.0★) — while the rest range from a handful of reviews down to zero, including Aiuta, which has **zero Shopify reviews roughly 17 months after launch** despite being a recognized try-on technology brand elsewhere. Ratings cluster suspiciously near-perfect (96–100% five-star) across nearly every app, which is consistent either with genuine early-adopter satisfaction or with the well-known pattern of new app categories where dissatisfied merchants simply uninstall rather than leave a review — the research explicitly flags this as something to treat cautiously rather than as proof of quality. Marketing positioning is similarly undifferentiated: nearly every listing claims "photorealistic," "reduces returns," "boosts conversion," and "one-click, no-code install," with only Antla and the self-interested competitor Garu breaking from the pack to argue that rendering quality is now a commodity and that size-accuracy is the real remaining battleground ([Garu blog](https://www.garu.ai/blog/best-shopify-virtual-try-on-apps)). No confirmed shutdowns or acquisitions were found in this specific segment during 2025–2026, reinforcing that the category is still in an active land-grab phase rather than a maturing, consolidating one.

## The rendering engine itself is not a competitive advantage

The single most consequential finding for TryFit's positioning is that its own underlying model family is not unique to it. **Antla — one of the two most established, best-reviewed apps in the category — is confirmed by FASHN's own blog to run on FASHN's TryOn API**, the same model family referenced in this project's own `RunpodVtonClient.php` and `fashn-vton-1-5` RunPod endpoint ([FASHN blog](https://fashn.ai/blog/add-a-clothing-tryon-feature-to-your-shopify-store-with-antla)). FASHN itself markets its API on "fabric drape" and "prints, logos, and text" fidelity "more faithfully than most competitors" — an implicit admission, from the model vendor's own framing, that generic diffusion try-on commonly struggles with exactly those details. Independent trade coverage corroborates this as a category-wide weak point: fabric transparency, reflective materials, unusual poses, heavy layering, and non-standard body shapes are repeatedly named as unresolved technical limitations across the space, not failures specific to any one vendor. The practical implication is direct: TryFit cannot credibly claim "our images look more realistic than Antla's" as a headline differentiator, because both are drawing on the same underlying generative technology and are subject to the same known failure modes. What TryFit *can* claim, and what the research surfaced no competitor claiming with comparable specificity, is garment-type-aware handling — this project's own recent engineering work on saree-specific prompts and flat-lay-to-model conversion addresses exactly the "one-size-fits-all garment problem" that the broader market appears not to have specialized against. That is a real, defensible technical differentiator precisely because the render engine itself is not one.

## Feature and pricing table stakes TryFit should benchmark against

Three feature dimensions recur across listings and mark out where TryFit needs to at least match the pack, plus one dimension — pricing architecture — where the entire category has converged on a single model. Lead capture is advertised by only three of twelve apps (Genlook, Antla, Icona), each describing it as woven into the try-on flow rather than a hard gate blocking the result; a real analytics dashboard with funnel/revenue-attribution language is claimed by just two (Genlook and Nivera), with the rest offering only vague "usage stats." A distinct merchant-side bulk content-generation studio (separate from the live shopper widget) is a genuine headline feature for only one app, Visualize AI, with Genlook and Icona offering thinner versions of the same idea. Widget customization is uniformly underdocumented — "custom branding" is the most repeated phrase in the category, but no listing describes granular controls like button placement or copy editing, and white-labeling (removing the vendor's own branding) is treated industry-wide as a paid-tier upsell rather than a default (Genlook gates it to its $99/mo Pro tier; Lit Outfit to its $29.99/mo Boutique tier and above). On pricing, every one of the eight target apps uses flat monthly subscription tiers with a bundled try-on/credit allowance and per-unit overage — essentially SaaS API-credit pricing — with entry-level paid tiers clustering tightly at **$19.99–$25/month**; no app was found using GMV or revenue-share pricing. Four apps (Genlook, Icona, Lit Outfit, Nivera) offer a genuinely free tier, but all are capped at roughly 10–25 try-ons/month, positioning them as demo tiers rather than viable production use — a gap TryFit could exploit or avoid replicating, depending on strategy. Camweara and Aiuta stand out for gating on product-catalog size rather than pure generation volume, which matters for large-catalog merchants with low per-SKU volume — a pricing angle none of the credit-only apps address.

## The concurrency problem, stated plainly

TryFit's own controlled load test — three simultaneous requests fired against the live `fashn-vton-1-5` RunPod endpoint at `workersMin: 1` / `workersMax: 1` — measured the queueing effect directly rather than estimating it (internal load test, `docs/concurrent-load-report.html`). At the API layer alone, generation time itself was nearly constant across all three shoppers (13.5–14.3 seconds each), which confirms the delay is entirely a queuing artifact, not a slower model: the 1st shopper saw a result in **15.18s**, the 2nd in **30.68s** (15.0s of that spent waiting for the single worker to free up), and the 3rd in **45.28s** (30.35s of waiting). Measured end-to-end in the real widget — including image upload, the Shopify App Proxy hop, and the Remix→PHP round trip — the numbers get worse: **27s, 44s, and 64s** for shoppers 1 through 3, meaning real-world overhead on top of the raw model adds another 12–19 seconds per shopper beyond the queuing delay itself. The team already proved this is fixable: raising `workersMax` from 1 to 10 let up to 10 shoppers generate in true parallel once RunPod's reactive autoscaling warmed additional GPU workers, and the same test harness captured the transition. The fix was then deliberately reverted — the user explicitly instructed "don't change the runpod maxworker i need it as maxworker = 1," prioritizing cost control, since RunPod bills per concurrently-running worker, over eliminating the queue. This is the crucial nuance for positioning: TryFit's concurrency limitation is not a capability gap versus competitors so much as an unverified cost trade-off that every commodity-API-based competitor in this category is presumably also making in some form — none of the 12 competitor apps researched had a single public review mentioning generation speed, timeouts, or queuing, positive or negative, meaning either their reliability is genuinely fine, their limits are more generous, their traffic is too low to expose the problem, or (per the reviews-and-reliability research) dissatisfied merchants simply aren't leaving reviews. TryFit is the only party in this comparison with hard, measured numbers on its own behavior under load; competitors have zero public data either way.

## Conclusion

The strategic reality is that TryFit is competing in a category where the underlying technology has commoditized faster than the market structure has consolidated — twelve apps, thin review bases, nearly identical marketing copy, and at least one established competitor (Antla) confirmed to run the same model family TryFit does. In that environment, positioning on "our renders look better" is not a credible or sustainable claim; positioning on garment-type specialization is, because it is the one dimension the research found no competitor addressing with comparable depth. The concurrency limitation deserves the same honesty internally that this report gives it: it is real, it is measurable (15–20 seconds of added wait per simultaneous shopper, worse at the actual widget layer than at the raw API layer), and it was a deliberate, reversible, cost-driven choice rather than a technical wall — which means it is also a decision the team can revisit with full knowledge of the trade-off rather than having to discover it under pressure. The sharper risk is timing: queuing delays bite hardest exactly when a store has simultaneous traffic, which is to say exactly when a merchant's marketing is working — the moment TryFit would most want to look fast is the moment `workersMax: 1` makes it look slowest. Given that no competitor has public data suggesting they've solved this any better, TryFit does not need to claim superiority on speed; it needs a considered answer for what happens at 3+ concurrent shoppers before that scenario becomes common enough that a merchant notices on their own.
